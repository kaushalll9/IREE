1 Web crawler: official NTA notices

The Reddit data ends in August 2022 and can't answer "when is the exam?". nta_crawler.py does a polite breadth-first crawl of the NTA sites. It collects every JEE/NEET notice, classifies each one by exam and type with ordered regular-expression rules, reads its upload date from the file name, and extracts the date lines from the PDF text. The chatbot routes official questions (an exam name plus an official topic such as admit card, result or last date) to the newest matching notice for the year asked.

1.3 Extracting dates from PDFs

NTA's important-dates tables have no ruling lines. pypdf's text extraction split them into roughly one word per line, so the row "Date of Examination 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 and 28 January 2027" was never extracted and the bot answered a 2027 question with a 2025 notice. Switching to pdfplumber, which keeps each row on one line, fixed this. Both libraries were run on the same 20 PDFs:

1.4 Publication dates

107 of 107 notices get an exact upload date from the NTA file name (/uploads/2026/04/20260409….pdf → 2026-04-09). Only 4 PDFs print a parseable "Dated:" line to check against: 50% match exactly; the others were uploaded 17, 16 days after the date printed on them (Syllabus for NEET (UG)-2026 Examination, Syllabus for NEET (UG) 2025 Examination ). So the stored date is the upload date, which is what "latest notice" needs.

2 Domain dictionary

2.1 The problem: vocabulary mismatch

The search engine (BM25F) finds posts that contain the same words as the question, after stemming. Students and posts often use different words for the same thing:

A student types……but many relevant posts sayKind of variation
pwphysics wallah, physicswallahabbreviation / brand spelling
hall ticketadmit cardsynonym
hcvhc vermaabbreviation of a book
boardsboard, cbseinflection / specific instance
unacademyunacdemymisspelling
josaajossa, counsellingmisspelling / process name

A general thesaurus such as WordNet knows none of this slang, abbreviations or Hinglish. The domain dictionary learns these equivalences from the 43,116 posts themselves and adds them to the query. This is called query expansion.

Step 1 · Tokenisation

Each post's title and body are lower-cased and split into word tokens with the pattern [a-z0-9]+('[a-z]+)?. Hinglish is kept as is ("kaise", "karu"). No stop words are removed, because Word2Vec needs the full context.

Step 2 · Phrase detection (collocations)

Many domain terms are multi-word ("physics wallah", "hall ticket", "jee mains"). gensim's Phrases joins two adjacent words into one token when they occur together far more often than chance:

score(a, b) = (count(a b) − min_count) × |V| ÷ (count(a) × count(b))   → join if score > 15
min_count = 10, |V| = vocabulary size; this is a PMI-style association score

The final vocabulary has 1,933 phrase tokens. Examples in the dictionary: jee_mains, jee_main, jee_advanced, admit_card, organic_chemistry, class_11, class_12, mohit_tyagi, physics_wallah, mock_test, neet_2022, mock_tests, take_a_drop, answer_key.

Step 3 · TF-IDF salience (the statistical part)

TF-IDF scores how characteristic a term is of a document: frequent in the post (TF), rare across posts (IDF). We use it to rank every term in the corpus by importance:

tfidf(t, d) = (1 + ln tf(t, d)) × (ln((1 + N) ÷ (1 + df(t))) + 1), each post vector L2-normalised
salience(t) = Σd tfidf(t, d)   → rank 1 = most salient
N = 43,116 posts; terms in fewer than 5 posts are dropped (11,559 terms ranked); scikit-learn TfidfVectorizer, sublinear tf

On its own, TF-IDF salience can't tell domain terms from chatter. The most salient terms overall are i, to, for, the, in, and, is, a, of, hai, jee, me, my, this, do, you. Restricted to dictionary terms, it ranks jee, neet, physics, jee_mains, 11th, maths, chemistry, marks, rank, 12th, mains, iit, allen, nta, pw, bitsat highest. So TF-IDF knows what matters, but not what means the same.

Step 4 · Word2Vec embeddings (the semantic part)

Word2Vec learns a vector for every word so that words used in similar contexts get similar vectors (the distributional hypothesis). "pw" and "physics wallah" appear around the same words ("lectures", "batch", "alakh sir"), so their vectors end up close together. We train the skip-gram variant: for each word, predict the words within ±5 positions, learning with negative sampling (5 random "wrong" words per real one). Similarity is the cosine of two vectors:

cos(u, v) = (u · v) ÷ (‖u‖ ‖v‖)   ∈ [−1, 1]
150 dimensions · window 5 · min_count 5 · 10 epochs · seed 42 · vocabulary 12,076 words (incl. phrases)

Step 5 · Seed terms and categories

92 hand-picked seed terms in 8 categories anchor the dictionary. The embeddings then discover the rest automatically. Only 5 categories are expanded: in subject, topic and institute, the nearest neighbours are siblings (chemistry → physics, iit → nit), not synonyms, and adding them would change the question's meaning.

2.4 How a query is expanded (expand_query())

  1. Split the query into words and adjacent word pairs ("hall ticket" → hall_ticket). A matched pair uses up its words, so "physics wallah" is not also treated as "physics".
  2. Look each one up in the dictionary. A hit pulls in its seed and the seed's top 3 neighbours.
  3. Drop a candidate term if it is already in the query, is another seed (allen → aakash would change the question), contains digits ("2024tard"), ranks outside the TF-IDF top 3,000, or has similarity below 0.6.
  4. Give each added term weight 0.5 × similarity. The student's own words keep weight 1.0, so expansion can nudge the ranking but never overpower the question.

Inside BM25F, the weight multiplies the term's score:

score(D, Q) = Σt ∈ Q w(t) × IDF(t) × tf̃(t, D) × (k₁ + 1) ÷ (tf̃(t, D) + k₁)
tf̃ = 2.5 × normalised title tf + 1.0 × normalised body tf; k₁ = 1.2, b = 0.75; w(t) = 1 for query words, 0.5 × cosine for added words

2.5 Key concepts

ConceptWhat it means here
Query expansionAdding related terms to a query to find relevant posts that use different words. It improves recall, at the risk of drift.
Vocabulary mismatchThe question and a relevant post describe the same thing with different words (pw / physics wallah).
Query driftExpansion adds a term that changes the question's meaning ("answer key challenge" + "official answer" pulls in unrelated posts). Precision drops.
Distributional hypothesisWords that occur in similar contexts have similar meanings. This is the basis of Word2Vec.
Word embedding (Word2Vec, skip-gram)A dense vector per word, learned by predicting nearby words, so that similar words are near each other.
Negative samplingTraining trick: instead of scoring every word in the vocabulary, push the real context word up and 5 random words down.
Cosine similarityThe angle between two vectors (1 = same direction). It measures how similar two words are.
Collocation / phrase detectionFinding word pairs that occur together more than chance (PMI-style score) and treating them as one term.
TF-IDFTerm frequency × inverse document frequency: a term matters if it is frequent in a post but rare across posts.
Sublinear TF, smoothed IDF1 + ln(tf) stops very repetitive posts from dominating; +1 smoothing avoids division by zero.
SalienceA term's total TF-IDF weight over the corpus: how important it is to this corpus. Used here as a filter.
Paradigmatic vs syntagmatic relationsWord2Vec finds words that can replace each other (pw ↔ physics wallah); TF-IDF co-occurrence finds words that appear together (josaa ↔ documents). This is why TF-IDF alone gives few synonyms.
Seed-based lexicon inductionStarting from a few hand-picked terms per category and growing the dictionary automatically from the data (weak supervision).
Intrinsic vs extrinsic evaluationIntrinsic: are the dictionary entries correct? Extrinsic: does search get better with them? Both are reported below.
Tuning / test splitSettings are chosen on 24 tuning queries; results are reported on 56 unseen test queries, so they are not overfitted.

2.7 How it was evaluated

Three ways of building the dictionary were compared. All use the same seeds, categories and expansion rules:

VariantHow related terms are found
TF-IDF only (co-occurrence)Terms whose TF-IDF document vectors are most similar to the seed's (they occur in the same posts). Statistics only. Cosines are rescaled onto the same 0.6–1.0 weight range.
Embeddings only (Word2Vec)Word2Vec nearest neighbours (the original dictionary).
Hybrid (Word2Vec + TF-IDF)Word2Vec neighbours kept only if TF-IDF ranks them in the top 3,000 (the current dictionary).
  • Intrinsic: every (seed → added term) pair a variant can put into a query was graded same meaning (synonym, abbreviation, spelling variant), related or unrelated, for 247 pairs across the variants (183 unique; evaluation/dictionary_pairs.json, graded by Claude).
  • Extrinsic: search quality on the 56 test queries with graded relevance judgments: NDCG@10, P@5, MAP, MRR and Success@5, plus a paired randomization test against no expansion. Settings (TF-IDF threshold 0.1, salience cut-off 3,000) were tuned on the 24 tuning queries only.

2.11 What the numbers mean

  • The hybrid builds the most accurate dictionary. 57% of its additions mean the same as the seed, against 34% for Word2Vec alone; unrelated additions fall from 20% to 9%. Rare neighbours are where Word2Vec is least reliable (few training examples), and TF-IDF salience removes exactly those.
  • Search quality differences are within noise. All variants are within about ±0.01 NDCG@10 of each other and of no expansion, and no p-value is near 0.05. Expansion fires on only 17 of 56 test queries, and most of them already match their posts word for word. The hybrid is best with BM25 and hurts the fewest queries; in the deployed system TF-IDF only happens to score highest on the test split, while Word2Vec only scored highest on the tuning split.
  • TF-IDF alone finds context, not synonyms. Only 1% of its additions are synonyms, but topical neighbours (josaa → documents, round) still help some queries, like pseudo-relevance feedback.
  • Where expansion helps: abbreviations and brand names (PW → physics wallah), synonyms (hall ticket → admit card) and Hinglish questions with English posts (boards aur jee → board, cbse).

2.13 Limitations and next steps

  • The pair grades and relevance labels were made by Claude (an LLM); a human review of a sample would make them more reliable.
  • Some kept expansions are still debatable: ncert → hc verma, rd sharma (other books), percentile → score.
  • Expansion is query-independent: "boards" always adds "cbse", even when a student means a state board.
  • Seeds are hand-picked; new slang (e.g. new coaching batches) needs a new seed or a retrain.
  • The data ends in August 2022, so newer terms are missing until the corpus is updated.
  • Next steps: per-category thresholds, judging more expansions by hand, and letting the semantic model veto expansions that move the query's sentence embedding too far.

4 Method and caveats

  • Who labelled the data. Relevance judgments: 1,863 by Claude (an LLM, graded against each query's stated intent) and 143 from the original project. The notice labels, the dictionary-pair grades and the official-question expectations were also made by Claude. All are saved in evaluation/ and should be spot-checked by a person (python evaluate.py annotate --review).
  • Tuning vs testing. Every tuned setting (RRF weight, TF-IDF threshold, salience cut-off) was chosen on the 24 tuning queries; all reported search numbers are on the 56 test queries.
  • Unjudged documents are skipped (condensed rankings), not counted as irrelevant; every system's top 10 is 99–100% judged.
  • Small differences are noise. With 56 test queries, differences under ~0.03 NDCG@10 are not significant.
  • Crawler accuracy after fixes is optimistic, because the fixes were made using the same labels and questions. The before-fix numbers (81.3% type accuracy, 90% official answers) are the unbiased ones.
  • Data age. The Reddit data ends in August 2022; official dates come from the live NTA crawl.
